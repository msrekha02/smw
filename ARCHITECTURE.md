# Architecture

Smart Market Watchlist — design rationale, system structure, and the reasoning
behind every decision that is not obvious from the code.

`README.md` is how to run it and what it does. This document is why it is built
the way it is. Where the two disagree, the code is the authority and both
documents are wrong.

## 1. The problem

A conventional watchlist is a table of percentages that resets at midnight. It
fails in four distinct ways, and every one of them is a failure of *framing*
rather than of data quality.

**A red day is not information.** When the market falls 5%, twenty of twenty
holdings turn red. Nothing on that screen distinguishes a stock that fell
because everything fell from one that fell for its own reasons. The user is
shown twenty numbers and learns nothing.

**"Today" is the wrong window.** If you last looked on Friday and it is now
Tuesday, a "today" percentage answers a question you did not ask. The window
that matters is the one that matches your absence, and that window is different
for every user and every visit.

**Size is not surprise.** A 3% move in a utility that normally moves 0.5% a day
is a genuine event. A 3% move in a small-cap semiconductor is a Tuesday. Sorting
by percentage change ranks the second above the first.

**A round trip is invisible to endpoint comparison.** A stock that spiked 12% on
Tuesday and gave it all back by Friday shows a ~0% weekly change. For someone
who was away all week, that is a total miss of a real event, and no consumer
watchlist catches it.

The product's answer to all four is the same sentence: **surface what is
statistically unusual for that specific stock, since you last looked, net of
what its sector did — and say why in one line.**

## 2. What the system claims

Four claims, each of which is asserted by a test rather than described:

| Claim | Where it is asserted |
|---|---|
| A market-wide selloff produces one banner and no individual criticals | `tests/test_scenarios.py::test_market_wide_selloff_produces_a_banner_and_no_stock_criticals` |
| A single-name move on a flat sector produces exactly one critical | `tests/test_scenarios.py::test_single_name_selloff_on_a_flat_sector_is_critical` |
| A spike-and-revert is caught at roughly zero net return | `tests/test_scenarios.py::test_spike_and_revert_is_caught_by_path_at_zero_net_return` |
| Two email addresses are two fully isolated users | `tests/test_auth.py::test_two_users_watchlists_and_snapshots_are_fully_isolated` |

A fifth claim — that the alert rate is defensible — is not a test but a
measurement, published at `/calibration` and regenerated by
`python -m scripts.calibrate`.

## 3. Invariants

These hold everywhere in the system. Most of the design falls out of them.

**I1. Nothing intraday ever writes to a statistic.** Ticks, quotes and live
prices write only to `ticker_latest`. Baselines are written exclusively by the
nightly job from stored daily bars. This one rule means the λ constants
calibrated for daily data are dimensionally correct, and a corrupted tick cannot
poison a baseline shared by everyone watching that ticker. It is also what
allows the live sanity filter to be crude: the worst a bad tick can do is
produce one wrong card for one poll cycle, self-healing on the next.

**I2. All date arithmetic happens in America/New_York; storage is UTC.**
Slicing a window in UTC means a user acknowledging at 02:00 IST on Sep 5 is on
Sep 4 in ET and silently misses a full trading day.

**I3. Session lengths come from `exchange_calendars`, never from a hardcoded
23,400 seconds.** Half-days close at 13:00. Treating one as a full session
inflates z by roughly 35% on exactly the days when volume is thin and the
estimate is already fragile.

**I4. Ranking is on an absolute composite; direction is carried separately.**
Ranking a signed score sorts a −5σ crash below a +0.5σ drift, where nobody would
ever see it.

**I5. `docker compose up` with no API keys boots a fully working product.** This
is a hard requirement, not a convenience. Nothing downstream should ever be
blocked on market hours, a rate limit, or a provider outage — not the demo, not
the tests, not development.

**I6. Degradation, never rejection.** Out of seeding credits? The add succeeds
and the item reads "scoreable tomorrow". Benchmark missing? β falls back to 0
(not 1.0) and the card says confidence is reduced. Redis down? Reads fall
through to Postgres and the system is slower, not wrong.

**I7. Silence is reported, never assumed.** A stale baseline says how stale. An
unjudged critical scores `null` precision, not `1.0`. A quiet day produces a
sentence, not a blank screen.

**I8. The reason string is derived from quantities actually used in the score.**
The sentence cannot drift away from the ranking, because it is built from the
same `ScoreResult` the ranking read.

```
                                      │

                    │            providers/             │
                    │  twelvedata  history, catalog     │
                    │  finnhub     quotes, WS, earnings │
                    │  replay      fixtures (default)   │
                    └───────────────────────────────────┘
```

Five containers: `postgres`, `redis`, `api`, `worker`, `web`. The API and the
worker are the same image with different entrypoints, because they share the
entire `api/` package — configuration, calendar, clock, quota, scoring — and
splitting them would mean publishing that package or duplicating it.

**Why the worker uses `DATABASE_URL_DIRECT`.** Supabase's pooler runs pgbouncer
in transaction mode, where session-scoped behaviour (advisory locks, prepared
statements, `LISTEN`/`NOTIFY`) is unreliable. `db.use_direct()` is called once
at worker boot and sets the preference process-wide, which beats threading a
`direct` flag through every call site and getting it wrong in one of them.

**Why the locks live in Redis logical DB 1.** Under `allkeys-lru` the lock keys
are evictable, and stampede protection would silently stop working under memory
pressure — exactly when it is needed. `docker-compose.yml` configures the
instance `noeviction` with a 256 MB cap.

**Why one worker process and no leader election.** `pg_advisory_lock` is unsafe
through pgbouncer's transaction mode, and a pooler-safe lease is real work for
no benefit at this scale. A single worker sidesteps the problem entirely. Two
workers would double-fetch and double-spend quota, so this is a genuine
constraint, documented rather than hidden.

## 5. The two clocks

The system has two independent notions of time because it is answering two
different questions.

**"Unusual" is a claim about a distribution, and the distribution is a daily
object.** σ, β and the residual histogram are estimated from close-to-close
returns. They move once a night, after the US close, from data that has settled.

**"Since you last looked" is a per-user window that can be 90 seconds or 9
months.** It has nothing to do with calendar days, and everything to do with
when that specific user last acknowledged that specific ticker.

The bridge between them is `TradingCalendar.session_span()`, which measures
trading time between two instants in units of one full session:

```python
span = Σ over sessions s: overlap([t0, t1], s) / true_length(s)
```

Weekends and holidays contribute nothing. A half-day contributes a full 1.0 when
fully spanned, because a half-day is a whole session's worth of information —
just a shorter one. This is the same quantity whether the window is five minutes
or five weeks, which is what makes one dispersion formula correct for both.

### `api/clock.py` — the single source of "now"

Every module asks `clock.now()` rather than `datetime.now()`, so the replay
providers can move the whole system onto a fixture date. Three replay modes:

| `REPLAY_CLOCK` | Behaviour | Used by |
|---|---|---|
| `compressed` (default) | Maps the real 24-hour wall clock onto the scenario's trading session, so prices always move | The demo, at any hour |
| `passthrough` | Keeps the real time of day, moves only the date | Honest about closed markets |
| `pinned` | Freezes at `REPLAY_NOW` | The test suite |

Compression divides seconds-of-day by 86,400 and applies that fraction to the
session's true length, so a half-day compresses correctly too.

A consequence worth stating: **freshness is measured against `clock.now()`, never
against SQL `now()`.** A database `now()` does not follow the replay clock, and
every price would read as years stale.

## 6. Data model

Sixteen tables plus one view, in `db/001_schema.sql`. Every statement is
idempotent and the file is re-applied on every boot; `db/002_etf_names.sql`
backfills instrument names added after the fact. Migrations run through the raw
asyncpg connection using the simple query protocol, because asyncpg wraps
`execute` in a prepared statement and a prepared statement cannot carry multiple
commands.

### Identity and lists

| Table | Purpose | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| `app_users` | Per-user counters only | Supabase owns identity; this row holds the daily seeding allowance and timezone. Created on first sight rather than by a webhook that could be missed |
| `watchlist_items` | `(user_id, ticker)` | One watchlist per user. Adding multi-list later means adding a `watchlist_id` column, nothing more |
| `watchlist_snapshots` | The per-user, per-ticker checkpoint | `last_seen_price`, `last_seen_bench`, `last_seen_bench_ticker`, `last_seen_at`, `is_initial`. Composite FK to `watchlist_items`, so removing a ticker cascades |

### Instruments

| Table | Purpose | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| `symbol_catalog` | The whole US universe | One provider credit for the entire list, so search costs nothing per query. GIN trigram index on `ticker \|\| ' ' \|\| name` |
| `tickers` | Instruments this system actually tracks | `instrument_class` ∈ {stock, sector_etf, broad_etf}; `benchmark_ticker` resolved at seed; `status` ∈ {seeding, active, halted, unknown} |
| `sector_benchmarks` | Sector → ETF | Eleven rows, seeded by the schema file |
| `ticker_daily_bar` | Split-adjusted OHLCV | ~1,260 rows per ticker after seeding |
| `earnings_dates` | Backfill plus forward calendar | Widens σ; never adds score |
| `corporate_actions` | Detected restatements | Append-only; `acknowledged` flips when the user re-adds |

### Statistics and prices

| Table | Purpose | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| `ticker_baseline` | One row per ticker, written nightly | EWMA state, β, σ, `recent_daily` (JSONB, 30 rows), `residual_hist` (JSONB, 40 bins), `frozen_reason` |
| `ticker_latest` | The live price | Written by the WS flusher and the on-demand path. Carries the sanity filter's `pending_*` hold state and `extended_price` |
| `ticker_latest_v` | View adding `age_s` and `is_stale` | A `GENERATED ... STORED` column using `now()` is rejected by Postgres as non-immutable, and would freeze the value at insert time even if allowed |

### Jobs and accounting

| Table | Purpose |
|---|---|
| `job_run` | One row per nightly run date; idempotent by `run_date` |
| `job_item` | Per-ticker state within a run: pending / in_flight / done / failed, with attempt count |
| `provider_response_cache` | The raw provider payload, written **before** parsing. This is what makes the nightly job exactly-once on quota |
| `quota_ledger` | `(partition, usage_date) → spent`. Shared across API instances and the worker |
| `digest_feedback` | Impressions, click-throughs, fast dismissals and thumbs. Unique on `(user_id, ticker, shown_at)` |

### `recent_daily`, and why it carries its own 52-week extremes

Each of the 30 stored rows holds:

```json
{"d": "2025-08-29", "exc": -0.0182, "volr": 3.81, "close": 176.0,
 "high": 178.4, "low": 174.1, "hh": 189.2, "ll": 96.4}
```

`hh` and `ll` are the 52-week extremes **as they stood at the end of the prior
session**. A window that opens on day D must be measured against the record D
*inherited*. Comparing against a record D itself set makes the distance beyond
it identically zero and collapses a continuous breach signal into a binary
touch. Storing it per row makes the comparison exact for any window up to 30
days without a second query.

## 7. The baseline estimator

`worker/baseline/ewma.py`. Pure, no I/O, no clock.

### Two decay constants

```
λ_beta = 0.98    34-day half-life    beta is structural
λ_vol  = 0.94    11-day half-life    volatility regimes genuinely shift fast
```

A fast-decaying beta is mostly noise. A slow-decaying volatility misses regime
changes. They are estimating different kinds of thing and get different memories.

### Deviations are taken against the prior mean

```python
dx = r_stock - s.mean_ret        # PRIOR mean
s.mean_ret += a * dx
s.var_stock = λ * (s.var_stock + a * dx * dx)
```

Updating the mean first and then measuring deviation from the *updated* mean
shrinks every deviation by λ and biases variance low by λ² — about 12% at
λ = 0.94, which inflates every z-score by roughly 6%. That error pushes toward
over-alerting, which is the exact direction this product cannot afford.
`tests/test_ewma.py::test_deviations_are_taken_against_the_prior_mean` writes out
the wrong recursion and asserts the bias.

### Winsorisation

Excess returns are clipped at ±5 × σ_MAD before entering the variance recursion,
where σ_MAD is a 120-day rolling median absolute deviation × 1.4826.

One −20% day inflates σ by **+42% with winsorisation and +871% without** — an
order of magnitude. Without the clip, the estimator suppresses alerts for weeks
during exactly the period a stock is most interesting. The winsorised σ returns
to within 15% of its pre-shock level about 21 sessions later; both figures are
asserted, and the horizon is named rather than glossed.

Critically, `ewma_step` returns the **unwinsorised** excess. The caller feeds
that to the residual histogram, so clipping protects the variance estimate
without hiding genuine tails from the honest picture on the detail page.

### Shrinkage

```
w        = r² · n / (n + 60)
β_used   = clip(w · β_raw + (1 − w) · 1.0, 0, 3)

w_σ      = n / (n + 40)
σ_idio   = max(sqrt(w_σ · var_excess + (1 − w_σ) · 0.016²), 0.004)
```

Beta shrinks toward 1.0 weighted by **both** fit quality and sample size. A young
or poorly-explained regression should not hand the scorer a beta of 2.4.

The σ floor of 0.4%/day matters more than it looks. A utility with a genuine
0.25%/day idiosyncratic σ would score 4σ on a 1% move and dominate every digest.
`DUK` is kept in the fixture basket specifically to exercise this.

### Seeding from a plain sample

The first 250 returns initialise the state by ordinary sample statistics rather
than by running the EWMA from zero. This removes initialisation bias without a
1/(1−λⁿ) correction, and it matters asymmetrically: residual bias after 250 bars
is negligible at λ_vol = 0.94 but material at λ_beta = 0.98. Beta's slow decay is
exactly what makes it need a real seed. The seed hands the recursion the *raw*
beta, not the shrunk one — shrinkage exists to protect against a thin sample, and
250 bars is not thin.

### Broad ETFs

A broad ETF benchmarked against itself gives excess identically zero, so it could
never alert — silently, with no error and no null. The same bug made the regime
banner dead code, since SPY against SPY can never reach 1.5σ. `instrument_class`
= `broad_etf` pins β to 0 and scores the raw return.

### Five years of bars

`SEED_BARS = 1260` (~5 trading years) rather than 250, because Twelve Data
charges one credit per `/time_series` call regardless of `outputsize`. The extra
history does little for the EWMA — it decays regardless — but it makes the
residual distribution meaningful and gives `scripts/calibrate.py` real data.

## 8. The scoring pipeline

Four modules under `api/scoring/`, all pure: no database, no clock of their own.
`scripts/calibrate.py` and the scenario tests drive exactly this code path, which
is what makes the calibration page a measurement of the production scorer rather
than a model of it.

```
resolve_window()  →  signals.*  →  score()  →  reasons.build()
   window.py         signals.py    score.py      reasons.py
```

### 8.1 `window.py` — resolving "since you last looked"

Two rules do the work.

**Windows of 2+ trading days anchor to a close.** σ is estimated from
close-to-close returns, but `last_seen_price` is whatever the price was at 10:03
on a Tuesday. Measuring an intraday-anchored return against
close-to-close-calibrated volatility carries extra noise the denominator does not
know about, biasing z upward — more false positives, in the direction the design
least wants. "Since Friday's close, −6.2%" is both statistically consistent and a
better sentence.

**Dispersion never reaches zero.** A same-session recheck spans a fraction of a
day; `σ · sqrt(0)` would produce 0/0 on every row. `MIN_N_EFF = 0.05` floors it.

Three branches:

| `sessions_between(last_seen, now)` | Anchor | Reference price |
|---|---|---|
| > 30 | Close, 30 sessions ago | Stored close on that session, **and** `n_days` capped to 30 |
| ≥ 2 | Close of the session on or before `last_seen` | Stored close; falls back to the checkpoint price with a note if absent |
| 0 or 1 | The user's own checkpoint | `last_seen_price`; benchmark reassignment mid-window is detected and reported |

The 30-day cap moves the price *and* the day count together. Capping `n_days`
alone would inflate z by sqrt(actual/30).

`Window.same_day` deliberately compares calendar dates rather than reading
`n_days`. `sessions_between` counts closes crossed, which is zero both for a
recheck an hour later and for a Friday-evening checkpoint viewed on Tuesday when
Monday was a holiday. Calling the second one "today" is simply wrong.

### 8.2 `signals.py` — the four signals

All four are built from **absolute** magnitudes with direction carried
separately (invariant I4).

**Window membership.** `window_days()` selects stored daily rows whose session
*close* falls in `(ref_at, now]`. Membership is decided by the close, not the
date, and that one rule is correct for a close-anchored window, a same-session
recheck and an overnight gap alike. Getting it wrong is expensive and quiet:
passing the whole 30-row history through unfiltered makes `z_path` report the
largest move of the last six weeks as if it happened while the user was away,
and every ticker becomes critical on the strength of an event they already saw.

**S1 — idiosyncratic displacement.**

```
excess  = (P₁/P₀ − 1) − β · (B₁/B₀ − 1)
σ_exp   = sqrt(σ_idio² · n_ordinary + (σ_idio · 2.0)² · n_earnings)
z_move  = excess / σ_exp
```

**S2 — path.** The largest single-day idiosyncratic move inside the window,
divided by σ_idio. `z_move` compares two endpoints, so a stock that spiked 12%
and round-tripped scores zero. For someone away a week that is a total miss.

**S3 — volume.** `clip(log₂(peak volume ratio), 0, 3)`. Doubling normal volume
scores 1; eight times scores 3. Returns the raw ratio too, because the ratio is
what the sentence says out loud.

**S4 — 52-week breach.** Continuous, not binary. Touching the record the window
*inherited* scores 1.0; every further σ beyond it adds a point, capped at 3. A
stock that pokes one cent above its old high and one that gaps 8% through it are
not the same event.

### 8.3 Earnings widens σ; it never adds score

This is the single most-revised decision in the system, and the reasoning is
worth stating in full.

An earlier version added a flat bonus when earnings fell in the window. That is
wrong twice. It **double-counts**: the price reaction *is* the surprise, and
`z_move` already contains it. And it has the **wrong sign**: a 3σ move on a
scheduled earnings day is *less* surprising than one on a random Tuesday, because
everyone knew a catalyst was landing.

Variance is additive across days, so the correct treatment is in the denominator:
earnings sessions contribute `(2σ)²` instead of `σ²`. Two tests pin this —
`test_earnings_widens_sigma_and_lowers_the_score` and
`test_earnings_adds_no_score_of_its_own`.

A missing earnings date makes a scheduled 4% move look like news, which is
precisely the false positive the design set out to remove. That is why the
forward calendar is refreshed weekly for the whole watched universe in one free
Finnhub call.

### 8.4 `score.py` — the composite and the gates

```
attention = 1.00 · min(|z_move|, 6)
          + 0.45 · min(z_path,  6)
          + 0.35 · vol_score
          + 0.40 · breach_score
```

| Tier | Threshold |
|---|---|
| critical | ≥ 3.00 |
| notable | 1.75 – 3.00 |
| minor | 0.90 – 1.75 |
| quiet | < 0.90 |

**Weights are in code, not in a database.** There is no `scoring_config` table.
A scoring change is a deploy, is reviewable in a diff, and is reproducible by
`scripts/calibrate.py`.

**Two gates sit beneath the statistics.** `MIN_N_EFF` floors the dispersion so a
recheck ten minutes into a session cannot divide by nearly zero. The
**material-move gate** then requires 0.5% of actual movement before anything can
be called critical or notable — an economic-significance floor beneath the
statistical one. Without it, a 0.4% move twelve minutes into a session divides by
a tiny sqrt(n_eff) and produces a spurious 4σ.

**The material-move gate reads the largest move anywhere in the window**, not
just the endpoint difference — see [§20.1](#20-departures-from-the-build-specification).

**A missing benchmark sets β to 0, never 1.0.** A silent beta substitution is the
likeliest source of a confident false critical: a β = 2.1 semiconductor name
scored at 1.0 during a −3% sector day shows a fabricated −3% of idiosyncratic
weakness. Instead the system reports `confidence: reduced` with the reason
"benchmark price unavailable; sector move not removed".

**Benchmark endpoints must match stock endpoints.** When the stock's own price is
an end-of-day close, the benchmark is read at the same `bar_date` via
`bench_close_on`. Otherwise you compute a stock return ending yesterday and
subtract a benchmark return ending now, attributing the entire current-day market
move to the stock as idiosyncratic.

Confidence drops to `reduced` for: a missing benchmark, a capped window, a stale
price, a frozen baseline, fewer than 200 sample days, a benchmark reassignment
mid-window, and a baseline more than two sessions behind.

### 8.5 `reasons.py` — the sentence

The reason string is the product. A number without a sentence is a number the
user has to interpret; the whole promise is that they do not have to.

Every clause is derived from a quantity that was used in the score, so the
sentence cannot drift away from the ranking. Three shapes:

**Round trip** (`|z_move| < 1` and `z_path ≥ 2`) gets its own sentence, because
it is the case `z_move` cannot see:

> Moved 5.9 sigma on Tuesday and gave it back, ending −0.9% against its sector
> since Friday's close, on 3.8x normal volume.

The "net" figure here is the **excess**, not the raw price change. Quoting the
raw change reads as a contradiction whenever the sector moved underneath it.

**Ordinary**: the headline is the price the user sees, and the σ clause is the
part that is net of the sector.

> Down 6.3% since Friday's close while tech was flat — 3.4 sigma weaker than its
> sector explains, on 3.9x normal volume.

Leading with one and sizing it by the other produces sentences like "Up 4.7%" on
a stock that fell 4.7%.

**Quiet**: absence of signal is reported as a finding.

> Nothing unusual. The biggest mover was AAPL, +0.8%, within its normal range.

Day names, not "yesterday". Tuesday's previous session is Friday when Monday was
a holiday, and "since yesterday" is simply false there.

Earnings appears as an *unscored context tag* — "earnings in the window" — which
explains why the bar was higher, not why the score was.

## 9. The worker

`worker/main.py` runs two schedulers in one process. They have different tick
rates, different data sources and different failure modes, but a second
deployable would buy nothing at this scale.

### 9.1 Bootstrap

Runs once at boot unless `SKIP_BOOTSTRAP=1`:

1. `sync_catalog()` — the whole US symbol universe, 2 catalog credits.
2. `seed_benchmarks()` — the 11 sector SPDRs plus SPY, **before anything else**.

Benchmarks seed first because a fresh deploy with no SPY bars would report
same-session scoring on every multi-day window, silently and confidently.
`bootstrap_state()` in the digest service enforces the other half: scored digests
are withheld until SPY has `SPY_MIN_BARS = 260` bars, and every card reads
`seeding` with an explanation.

### 9.2 The nightly job

Fires 20 minutes after the regular close, so late prints have settled. Idempotent
by `run_date`, so a restart mid-evening resumes rather than re-spends.

The split that makes recovery cheap: **fetch is quota-bound and non-idempotent;
compute is a pure function of stored bars.** Only the fetch half needs recovery
machinery, and it shrinks to "fetch bars, insert bars".

Per ticker, the order of operations is what makes a crash safe at every point:

```
a. response cache hit?            → skip the HTTP call entirely
b. mark in_flight, attempts += 1  [own transaction]
c. spend one credit, GET /time_series
d. write the raw response         [own transaction]
e. restatement? freeze, skip compute, done
f. upsert bars, recompute, done   [one transaction]
```

Each step owns its own transaction, because a single long transaction would roll
back the very bookkeeping recovery depends on.

**Exactly-once on quota.** The credit is spent at the HTTP call, *outside* any
transaction, so a ledger alone prevents reprocessing but not double-spending. The
raw response is therefore written to `provider_response_cache` **before** parsing,
and a retry checks the cache and skips the call. One window remains open: a crash
between (c) and (d) costs one credit for one ticker on one night. The ledger
over-counts there rather than under-counting — the safe direction against a hard
external cap — and
`test_a_crash_inside_the_http_call_overcounts_never_undercounts` asserts it
rather than hiding it.

**Enqueue order is descending refcount.** This matters for the failure mode, not
for throughput: a run that dies at 60% should leave the *least-watched* tickers
unrefreshed. `test_items_are_ordered_by_watcher_count` pins it.

The whole recovery contract is tested by killing the job at every step and
counting the resulting provider calls. Crashes at (b), (d), (e) and (f) each cost
exactly one call per ticker.

### 9.3 Corporate actions: detect and freeze, never auto-rebase

Bars are fetched with `adjust=splits`, so the provider restates history when a
split occurs and the divergence is detectable exactly: the close already stored
for a date no longer matches the close the provider returns for that date.
Tolerance is 0.5% — wider than rounding, narrower than any split.

On detection the system writes a `corporate_actions` row, sets
`ticker_baseline.frozen_reason`, and **touches no snapshot**. The card renders an
action panel telling the user to remove and re-add. Re-adding clears the freeze.

An earlier design classified the split from the restatement signature and rebased
user reference prices automatically. It was rejected on scope *and* on safety.
Any automated rebase built on a heuristic can, in its failure mode, silently
suppress the most important alert the product will ever generate.
Detect-and-freeze is roughly a tenth of the code and strictly safer; the cost —
the user must act — is stated in the UI rather than hidden.

Not touching snapshots is the point: the user's reference price is theirs, and
the only safe way to reset it is for them to do it.

### 9.4 The intraday layer — two tiers, not three

**Hot tier: WebSocket over the top 50 by watcher count.** Finnhub's free
WebSocket caps at 50 symbols, which is why membership is by refcount rather than
round-robin. Watchlists concentrate heavily in mega-caps, so 50 symbols covers a
large share of what anyone is actually looking at. Ties are broken by ticker so
membership is stable and does not churn subscriptions on every refresh.

Ticks are buffered — last tick wins per symbol — and flushed to `ticker_latest`
in one batched upsert every 5 seconds. A per-tick write would turn a busy open
into a write storm for data that is overwritten seconds later. Membership is
re-evaluated every 300 s; reconnection uses jittered exponential backoff from 1 s
to 30 s.

**Cold tier: on demand.** Everything outside the top 50 is fetched only when
somebody opens a list containing it, then cached for 60 s. Round-robin polling
would spend budget refreshing prices nobody is looking at.

Cost is **O(distinct tickers being viewed)**, never O(users × tickers): a ticker
on 400 watchlists costs one fetch per 60 s. The single-flight lock is what makes
that true under load.

**A middle tier was designed and cut.** It would have polled tickers watched by
someone-but-not-many at some intermediate cadence. It adds a third code path, a
third freshness label and a third failure mode to buy a modest improvement in a
narrow band of popularity. Two tiers with an honest age label is the better trade.

**Benchmarks are polled unconditionally, every 60 s.** They must be fresher than
the stocks they anchor: a stale benchmark mis-attributes a sector move to the
stock, which is the one error this whole system exists to avoid. Twelve symbols a
minute against a 45/min budget is affordable precisely because it is a fixed cost
that does not grow with users. One extra pass fires after the close to pin the
settled price; after that, polling a closed market spends budget to learn nothing.

### 9.5 The sanity filter

A print is implausible only if it disagrees with **both** the last good price and
the previous close by more than 20%. A stock that gapped overnight disagrees with
`prev_close` but not with the last good print; a genuine intraday halt-and-reopen
is the other way round.

A suspicious print is **held, not dropped**. Held prices surface as `verifying`,
the same suspicious level twice running is treated as news rather than
corruption, and the hold times out after 180 s so a real gap eventually gets
through.

The filter is allowed to be crude, and that is a consequence of invariant I1
rather than an oversight. A filter that also had to protect a shared estimator
would need a threshold tight enough to catch corruption and loose enough not to
quarantine real news, and no single value does both.

### 9.6 Extended hours

Extended-hours prices are **displayed, never scored**. After-hours volatility
needs its own calibration, and a thin 04:00 print would score 3σ on no volume.
But suppressing it entirely means the system looks quietest at the most
information-dense moment there is. The compromise: `ticker_latest.extended_price`
carries the print, `ticker_latest.price` holds the last scoreable close, and the
card reads `AH −8.2% · not scored until the open`.

## 10. Providers

Two provider interfaces, because no free provider does both halves well.

**Finnhub's `/stock/candle` returns 403 on free keys.** Historical OHLCV is a
premium product there, so history cannot come from Finnhub at all. That single
fact is why this system has two providers.

**Twelve Data charges one credit per `/time_series` call regardless of bar
count** (up to 5,000) and supports `adjust=splits`. Five years of split-adjusted
daily bars costs the same as one day.

| | Twelve Data | Finnhub |
|---|---|---|
| Role | The slow clock | The fast clock |
| Free tier | 800 credits/day, 8/min | 60 REST calls/min (metered at 45) |
| Provides | Daily bars, symbol catalog, market state | Real-time quotes, WebSocket (50 symbols), sector profiles, both earnings endpoints |
| Rate limiter | Sliding-window minute bucket | Token bucket |

Sector vocabulary differs: `FINNHUB_SECTOR_ALIASES` maps 30-odd Finnhub industry
strings onto the eleven sectors this system benchmarks against.

Finnhub's `/stock/profile2` returns nothing for ETFs, so the entire benchmark
universe would carry a `NULL` name forever. `ETF_NAMES` and `ETF_SECTORS` in
`api/config.py` are the source of truth for those seventeen instruments — fixed
instruments chosen by this system, not user input, so naming them in code is the
honest fix. `db/002_etf_names.sql` backfills pre-existing rows from the same
list, and `tests/test_integrity.py::test_the_etf_name_backfill_matches_the_static_map`
fails if the two ever drift apart.

### The replay providers

`PROVIDER_MODE=replay` is the default and is a hard requirement (invariant I5).

`ReplayHistory` serves deterministic bar fixtures and mirrors Twelve Data's
response shape exactly — newest first, every field a string — so the parse path
under test is the production parse path. It only serves bars up to the current
replay session; a fixture file holds the scenario's future too, and serving it
would let the nightly job see tomorrow.

`ReplayQuotes` generates the intraday path as a **Brownian bridge** from the
previous close to the session's actual close, seeded by
`blake2b(ticker:session)`. Two consequences: the price at 11:04 is identical on
every machine and every run, and the bridge lands exactly on the fixture's close,
so the live path and the stored bar never disagree.

If live mode is selected but a key is missing, that half falls back to replay and
says so in `/healthz`, rather than booting a half-dead system.

### Why the fixtures are synthetic

Redistributing Twelve Data or Finnhub history would breach both free tiers'
terms. `scripts/record_fixtures.py` generates 1,300 sessions for 65 instruments
from a fixed seed (`20240917`) through the same factor structure the scorer
assumes it is fitting:

```
r_sector = β_sm · r_market + ε_sector
r_stock  = β    · r_sector + ε_stock
```

with **Student-t (df=4) innovations** so the tails are fat and the calibration
page has something honest to report, and a slow multiplicative volatility regime
so EWMA vol has something to track. Betas are recoverable, which is what makes
`test_seed_beta_matches_ols_over_the_same_window` meaningful rather than
circular.

Every path is rescaled so its **last** close lands on a plausible present-day
price. Anchoring the end rather than the start keeps five years of fat-tailed
compounding from producing a $2,400 NVDA in a demo that is otherwise trying to
look credible. Scaling is uniform, so every return — and therefore every beta,
sigma and residual — is untouched.

Scenario days are generated through the *same* factor structure. Drawing each
ticker independently would decouple the ETFs from their constituents, the scorer
would correctly report that as a large idiosyncratic move, and the fixture would
manufacture criticals it never meant to.

## 11. Quota

Twelve Data's free tier is 800 credits/day. Without partitioning, one user adding
200 tickers starves baseline freshness for everyone.

```
800 credits/day
  640  RESERVED   nightly baselines
  120  SEEDING    new tickers, global queue, 20 per user per day
   40  CATALOG    symbol sync, market state, slack
```

**Enforcement is structural, not by convention.** `Budget.__init__` refuses to be
constructed against `RESERVED` without a worker-only sentinel object, and
`user_budget()` — the only budget a request handler may hold — cannot pass it. A
request path that tries raises `ReserveViolation`, which is a bug, not a rate
limit. The worker token is exposed as a *function* rather than a bare constant,
so a user-path import cannot accidentally grant access.

`test_a_user_path_cannot_construct_a_reserve_budget` and
`test_user_seeding_cannot_drain_the_nightly_reserve` assert both halves.

**Spending goes through the database ledger**, so two API instances and the
worker cannot each believe they have 640.

**Partial grants are deliberate.** A nightly run that can only refresh 400 of 600
tickers should refresh 400, in refcount order, not zero.

**The per-user allowance is separate from the global partition on purpose.** The
global partition protects the system; the 20/user/day allowance protects other
users from one enthusiastic one. It resets lazily on first use each day, in the
same `UPDATE` that charges it, so there is no reset job to miss.

**Adding an already-seeded ticker costs zero credits**, and watchlists concentrate
in mega-caps, so most adds are free.

Seeding runs in a FastAPI background task because it is network-bound, not
compute-bound: 1,259 EWMA iterations run in under 10 ms, but the 8 credits/min
ceiling means ~7.5 s per novel ticker.

## 12. Identity and isolation

There are exactly two identity paths and **one is live at a time**.

| `SUPABASE_JWKS_URL` | Token issuer | Verification | `POST /api/auth/login` |
|---|---|---|---|
| unset | Local HS256, any email | HS256 against `DEV_AUTH_SECRET` | 200 |
| set | Supabase Auth | RS256/ES256 against the project's JWKS | **404** |

Setting the JWKS URL flips the whole system in one move and takes the local
issuer offline in the same move, so a deployment cannot be talked into accepting
a token it minted for itself. The local issuer additionally refuses to run under
`PROVIDER_MODE=live`, announces itself on the login screen, and reports itself in
`/healthz` as `auth_mode: dev-hs256`.
`test_configuring_supabase_takes_the_local_issuer_offline` asserts the switch in
both directions.

**Why a local issuer exists at all.** `docker compose up` with no keys has to
boot a fully working product (invariant I5), and JWKS verification cannot work
without a project. It is a first-class path with its own `/login` screen rather
than a hidden fallback, because the per-user isolation claims are not
demonstrable without one.

**Any email is accepted deliberately.** This is a demonstration of per-user state
isolation, not of a login wall: the point is that two addresses get two
watchlists, not that a password was checked. Pretending otherwise would be
theatre, and the login screen says so in as many words.

**The dev user id is derived, not stored:** `uuid5(NAMESPACE_URL, "smw-dev:" +
lowercased email)`. The same address is therefore the same account across a
`docker compose down -v`, and `scripts/seed_demo.py` and a browser sign-in land
on the same row without coordinating.

**`app_users` is created on first sight**, both at sign-in and on the first
authenticated request, rather than by a webhook that could be missed. Supabase
owns identity; this table holds only what the system needs.

Isolation is enforced by `user_id` in every WHERE clause, and asserted end to end
by `test_two_users_watchlists_and_snapshots_are_fully_isolated`, which fails if
that predicate is ever dropped. `scripts/acceptance.ps1` walks the same claim
against a running stack over HTTP.

## 13. The digest token and read receipts

### The token

An HMAC-SHA256-signed payload carries the observed prices to the client and back,
so an acknowledgement checkpoints **exactly the numbers the user looked at**
rather than whatever the price is at ack time. There is no server-side storage,
so the core mechanic has no Redis dependency.

**Per-ticker `at` timestamps**, because a digest mixes a WebSocket price from two
seconds ago with a cold-tier close from yesterday. Checkpointing both at a single
digest-level timestamp leaves the cold one's price stale while its timestamp
claims *now*, and the next visit measures a diff that never happened.

Verification checks signature, expiry (30-minute TTL) **and caller identity**.
Cross-user reuse is rejected in the token rather than left to the ack `UPDATE`'s
`user_id` predicate: a token that silently checkpoints nothing looks identical to
a working one from the client's side.

`DIGEST_TOKEN_SECRET` must come from the environment and is never generated at
boot, because one API instance issues a token and another has to verify it.

### Acknowledgement

Acks are **monotonic per ticker** — `WHERE last_seen_at < :at` — which makes them
idempotent and order-independent. Two devices can ack in any order, the later
observation wins, and an old tab cannot rewind the checkpoint.

The response reports **per-ticker outcomes** (`applied` / `superseded` /
`not_found`), because a silent zero-row update returning a bare 200 produces a
quiet re-ack loop that looks exactly like broken receipts from the client's side.

### The client rules

A card marks read after **2 continuous seconds at ≥50% visibility**, and the
observer **does not arm until the first user interaction** (scroll, pointer, key,
wheel, touch). Arming on load would route around "never ack on page load" through
the viewport path: on a short list where everything fits on screen, the whole
digest would ack while the reader was still orienting.

**Critical cards never auto-ack.** They require an explicit dismissal, because
that is where a wrongly-cleared diff costs the most trust. The rule is enforced
in the store rather than in each caller.

Receipts batch for 5 s and flush on `visibilitychange → hidden` and `pagehide`
using `fetch(..., {keepalive: true})` — **not** `sendBeacon`, because a beacon
cannot set an `Authorization` header and so could not authenticate at all.

If a tab dies before the flush, receipts are lost and the cards reappear. Showing
something twice is an annoyance; hiding a critical nobody read is the failure that
loses trust permanently. On a normal flush, only tickers the server reports as
`applied` or `superseded` are cleared; a `not_found` stays pending so a transient
race does not silently drop the receipt.

Sign-out clears the token and the receipts together, because receipts are keyed
to a digest issued for one user and carrying them across a sign-in would ack one
person's cards against another's checkpoints.

Nine Vitest cases cover exactly these rules in `web/tests/receipts.test.ts`.

## 14. Caching, ETags and stampede control

**Every Redis call degrades to a no-op if Redis is unreachable.** A 5-second
circuit breaker prevents hammering a dead instance. Redis being down must make
the system slower, not wrong: reads fall through to Postgres and acks are
untouched.

**Single-flight.** `SET NX` around each quote fetch, so 400 simultaneous requests
on one expired popular key produce exactly one provider call. Losers wait on the
holder for up to 2 s rather than queueing their own call; if nothing is published
in time they return `None` and the caller serves whatever it already has.
Blocking a user's digest on a provider is worse than showing them a price with an
honest age on it. Release uses a Lua compare-and-delete so a slow fetch whose
lock expired cannot delete the next holder's.

`test_concurrent_requests_on_one_expired_key_make_one_provider_call` runs 200
concurrent requests and asserts exactly one call, with every caller served the
same price.

**The cache-hit write-through.** The digest reads prices from `ticker_latest`,
not from Redis. A row that went missing while the cache was still warm would show
`no_data` for a full TTL and never self-heal — the refresh would find the value in
Redis, return happily, and write nothing. `fetch_one(ensure_db=True)` writes a
cache hit through to the table. It costs one upsert, and only in the recovery
case.

**ETag / 304.** The client polls every 20 s while the market is open, and most of
those polls have nothing to say. The ETag is computed over a **stabilised** body
with `token`, `as_of`, `next_poll_after_ms`, per-card `observed_at` and the
volatile freshness fields removed — the token is freshly signed on every request
and `as_of` ticks every second, so hashing the raw response would make 304
unreachable.

On a 304 the client keeps the digest it has, **including the token**, so pending
acks stay valid.

**The server sets the cadence.** `next_poll_after_ms` and the
`X-Next-Poll-After-Ms` header carry 20 s open / 15 min closed. The client does
not guess.

## 15. The web application

Next.js 15 App Router, React 19, TanStack Query, Zustand, Tailwind, Recharts.

### Route structure

```
app/
  layout.tsx           fonts + session provider, nothing else
  page.tsx             the public landing page (server component, no data fetching)
  (app)/
    layout.tsx         nav + RouteGuard + ErrorBoundary + footer
    login/             sign-in
    digest/            the product
    watchlist/         add, remove, search
    ticker/[ticker]/   the page that shows its work
    calibration/       predicted vs observed
```

The authenticated chrome lives one level down in the `(app)` route group rather
than in the root layout, because the marketing page has to render with no session
and with a nav of its own. The route group adds no path segment, so `/digest` is
still `/digest`.

`RouteGuard` sits in the group layout once rather than in each page: **a new
route is protected by existing rather than by remembering to add a check.**
`/calibration` is explicitly public because it holds no user data — the rates are
a replay of stored bars, and precision@critical aggregates every vote in the
system rather than yours. It is also the strongest evidence the project has, so
putting it behind a sign-in would hide the one page that proves the claim.

The landing page is a server component that fetches nothing. Every figure on it
traces to this document or to the calibration artifact. A marketing page that can
be down because Postgres is down is worse than one that is merely static.

### Session handling

A token in `localStorage` is a claim, not a session. `AuthProvider` asks
`/api/auth/me` before letting anything render, so an expired or foreign token
fails on boot rather than on whichever page happens to need data first. An
expired `exp` claim is detected locally first to skip a request that is certain to
401 — politeness, not a check; the server remains the authority.

A 401 anywhere in the app clears the token and broadcasts, so one expired token
cannot leave the app half signed-in. A `storage` listener covers a second tab.

`OfflineError` and `ApiError` are distinct types, because a dead network is a
different state from a server that said no and the UI shows them differently.

### The three charts

Each exists because a number alone would not be checkable.

**Expected-range cone** (`ExpectedRangeCone.tsx`). The cone is `σ_idio · sqrt(t)`
— the same dispersion the scorer divides by — so a line leaving the outer cone is
exactly a |z| above 2 and the picture cannot disagree with the number beside it.
The path is sliced **server-side** by `signals.window_days`, the function the
scorer uses; re-deriving that slice in the client would put a chart on screen
that quietly disagrees with the score.

**Decomposition waterfall** (`DecompositionWaterfall.tsx`). Raw move, minus the
part the sector explains, leaves the idiosyncratic remainder. Only the remainder
is scored, so it carries the weight, and the normal range for the window is drawn
as a band through its row: the overshoot past the band is the finding, visible
without reading a number.

**Residual histogram** (`ResidualHistogram.tsx`). The fat-tails table asserts the
tails are heavier than a normal; this is that assertion drawn, with the normal
scaled to the same area so the two are directly comparable rather than merely
adjacent.

The decomposition panel's contributions sum to the attention score **exactly**
(`test_contributions_sum_to_attention`), so the arithmetic on screen is the
arithmetic that produced the ranking.

### Other deliberate choices

- `QuietTable` virtualises: a 100-ticker watchlist should not render 100 rows to
  show eight.
- `ErrorBoundary` wraps every page, so one broken render cannot blank the app.
  React has no hook equivalent; an error boundary must be a class.
- Symbol search debounces at 150 ms against the **local** trigram index, so it
  stays sub-millisecond and keeps working during a provider outage.
- `instrument_class` is never shown. A broad ETF reads "Broad market"; a sector
  ETF reports the sector it *is*.
- Entrance animations use `animation-fill-mode: both`, not `forwards`. With a
  stagger delay and forwards-only fill, the element paints at natural opacity for
  the length of the delay and then snaps to zero, which reads as a flicker.
- `prefers-reduced-motion: reduce` disables animation *and* restores the end
  state, because `.animate-in` starts at opacity 0 and would otherwise never
  appear.

## 16. Validation and calibration

Everything else in this system validates the model against itself. Two numbers on
`/calibration` do not.

### The alert rate

`python -m scripts.calibrate` replays 252 sessions of stored bars for a 40-ticker
basket through `api.scoring.score.score` — **the same function the digest
endpoint calls, not a model of it.** The script contains no scoring logic of its
own; a calibration number produced by a parallel implementation would measure the
wrong thing.

10,080 checks in the shipped artifact:

| | Predicted (normal null) | Observed |
|---|---|---|
| Critical per week | 1.62 | 6.27 |
| Notable per day | 3.81 | 2.78 |

"Predicted" holds the volume and breach terms at their **observed** values, keeps
the same material-move gate and the same tiering, and replaces only the return
distribution with a standard normal. That isolates exactly one assumption —
Gaussian residuals — rather than comparing against a hand-derived figure that
shares none of the scorer's gates.

The gap is the fat-tail correction, and the tails are where you would expect:

| | Observed | Under a normal |
|---|---|---|
| Residual sd | 1.065 | 1.000 |
| Kurtosis | 16.65 | 3.00 |
| P(\|z\| > 2) | 5.71% | 4.55% |
| P(\|z\| > 3) | 1.65% | 0.27% |

Real returns are fat-tailed, so the realised critical rate runs about four times
the Gaussian prediction. **That is reported, not corrected for.** Replacing the
Gaussian z with the empirical ECDF is designed and deliberately not built; the
residuals are collected, stored per ticker and displayed, and the detail page
says so in as many words.

### precision@critical

The fraction of critical cards a human actually agreed with, computed from
`digest_feedback`. A thumb is an explicit signal; a click-through is a weak
positive; a fast dismissal (under 3 s) is a weak negative. They are reported
separately rather than blended, because an unlabelled composite would be exactly
the kind of number this page exists to avoid.

With no thumbs yet it is `null` rather than `1.0`: **an unjudged critical is not
a correct one.** This is the only number in the system that checks whether
"meaningful" is meaningful to a person.

The feedback window uses `clock.now()`, not the wall clock — feedback rows are
stamped with the system clock, and under the replay providers a wall-clock window
would silently exclude every row.

## 17. Failure modes

| Failure | Behaviour | User sees |
|---|---|---|
| Worker crash | Resumes from ledger + response cache, no re-spend | Nothing, unless the baseline is more than 2 sessions old |
| Nightly job incomplete | Retries, resumes next night, least-watched left last | `reduced` + "baseline last updated Sep 2" |
| Fresh deploy, no SPY bars | Scored digests withheld until 260 bars | All tickers `seeding`, with the reason |
| WebSocket drops | Jittered backoff reconnect; symbols age into the cold tier | "live" → "~1 min" |
| Finnhub 429 or down | Token bucket backs off; quotes go stale | Freshness label lengthens |
| Twelve Data quota exhausted | Seeding pauses; the reserve is untouched | New adds read `seeding` |
| Cache stampede | Single-flight lock | One provider call, all callers served |
| Redis down | Circuit breaker; reads fall through to Postgres | Slower only; acks unaffected |
| Benchmark stale or missing | β falls back to **0**, not 1.0 | `reduced` + "sector move not removed" |
| Implausible tick | Held one cycle, timeout release after 180 s | `verifying` |
| History restated | Baseline frozen, alerts paused, no snapshot touched | Action card with instructions |
| Ticker halted / delisted | Status flip | "no recent trades" / "symbol no longer resolves" |
| Ack superseded | Per-ticker result in the response | Nothing; no retry loop |
| Receipts lost to a tab crash | Cards reappear | The item is shown again |
| Row missing but cache warm | `ensure_db` write-through repairs it | Nothing |
| Benchmark reassigned mid-window | Detected, reported | `reduced` + "benchmark changed XLK → XLC mid-window" |

## 18. Scale and licence ceilings

Expensive work is keyed by ticker; cheap work is keyed by user.

```
nightly quota  = O(distinct active tickers)         not O(users)
intraday quota = O(distinct tickers being viewed)   not O(users × tickers)
digest read    = O(list size), no writes, 304 when nothing changed
```

The digest read path is one SQL join plus a vectorised pass over at most 100
rows, with no writes and no avoidable provider calls. That is what keeps p50 in
the tens of milliseconds.

**Technical ceiling:** roughly 600 tickers with maintained baselines (640 nightly
credits, one per ticker) and 50 with real-time pricing (the WebSocket cap) — about
1,000–5,000 users depending on watchlist overlap.

**The licence ceiling is lower and binds first.** Finnhub's free tier is personal,
non-commercial use, and Twelve Data's free Basic tier carries similar limits. This
is a prototype running under personal-use licences. A production deployment needs
commercial agreements, and the likely move is consolidating onto Twelve Data,
which costs the free WebSocket and turns the hot tier into polling.

**Scope is US-listed equities and ETFs**, enforced at add time against the local
catalog rather than failing later, because both free tiers are US-only.

## 19. How the build evolved

The design did not arrive finished. These are the changes made *during* the build,
each because the first version was wrong in a way that only showed up once it ran.

**Earnings moved from the numerator to the denominator.** The first version added
a flat bonus when earnings fell in the window. It double-counted the surprise and
had the wrong sign. It now widens σ and adds nothing. See [§8.3](#83-earnings-widens-σ-it-never-adds-score).

**Corporate actions went from auto-rebase to detect-and-freeze.** The first design
classified the split from the restatement signature and rebased user reference
prices automatically. Cut on safety as much as scope: a heuristic rebase can, in
its failure mode, silently suppress the most important alert the product will ever
generate. See [§9.3](#93-corporate-actions-detect-and-freeze-never-auto-rebase).

**Broad ETFs got their own instrument class.** SPY benchmarked against SPY gives
excess identically zero, so a broad ETF could never alert — silently, with no
error and no null — and the market-regime banner was dead code for the same
reason. `broad_etf` now pins β to 0 and scores the raw return.

**52-week extremes moved into `recent_daily` rows.** Comparing against the
baseline's own `wk52_high` makes the distance beyond a new high identically zero.
Each row now carries the record it *inherited* (`hh`/`ll`).

**The same-session branch switched from `session_fraction_elapsed` to
`session_span`.** A 15:50 → 15:55 recheck spans five minutes of trading, not a
whole day. `session_fraction_elapsed` is still used, correctly, for
close-anchored windows and for the market-regime z.

**The material-move gate was widened to the whole window.** Gating on the endpoint
difference alone silenced the spike-and-revert case, which is the product's
headline demo.

**A sign-in UI was added, and the isolation claims became testable.** Before it
existed, per-user isolation was a claim about code nobody could exercise.
`tests/test_auth.py` and `scripts/acceptance.ps1` were written alongside it. The
`/api/dev/token` endpoint remains for `curl` and scripts; `POST /api/auth/login`
is the same issuer with a JSON body.

**A residual histogram was added to the calibration artifact.** The page
previously asserted fat tails with four summary numbers. The `residual_histogram`
field is optional in `CalibrationOut` precisely because artifacts generated before
it exists must still load — the page drops the chart rather than failing.

**ETF names and sectors became a static map plus a migration.** Finnhub returns
nothing for ETFs, so the whole benchmark universe carried `NULL` names. The fix
is `ETF_NAMES`/`ETF_SECTORS` in `api/config.py`, `db/002_etf_names.sql` for rows
that predate it, and an integrity test asserting the two never drift.

**`ensure_db` was added to the on-demand refresh path.** A `ticker_latest` row
that went missing while Redis was still warm showed `no_data` for a full TTL and
never self-healed. `test_a_cache_hit_still_repairs_a_missing_row` pins the fix.

**Per-ticker ack results replaced a bare 200.** A silent zero-row update looks
exactly like broken receipts from the client, and produced a quiet re-ack loop.

**The digest token gained per-ticker timestamps.** A single digest-level `as_of`
left cold-tier prices stale while their timestamp claimed *now*.

**A separate `smw_test` database was introduced.** Several integrity tests
truncate tables, and pointing them at the development database silently deleted
whatever you were looking at. `db/init/00_test_db.sql` creates it at cluster
init; `tests/conftest.py` defaults to it.

**The front end was rebuilt on a new design system.** The original palette —
dark navy `ink-*` tokens in `tailwind.config.ts` — was replaced by a CSS
custom-property system in `globals.css` (`--surface-*`, `--ink-*`, `--brand`,
glass panels, Inter + JetBrains Mono). Both are still present: the Tailwind
colour scale remains the source for class-based colours, while the CSS variables
carry the newer surfaces. The superseded versions of the landing page, the login
screen, the watchlist page and the old stylesheet are retained as comments in
their files rather than deleted, which is why several of those files are longer
than their live content.

**Bar fixtures were re-anchored to end prices.** Five years of fat-tailed
compounding from a fixed start produced implausible present-day prices. Rescaling
so the *last* close lands on a chosen price leaves every return, beta and residual
untouched.

**Scenario days were regenerated through the factor model.** Drawing each ticker
independently decoupled ETFs from their constituents and manufactured criticals
the fixtures never intended.

## 20. Departures from the build specification

Four places where the original written specification would have been wrong to
implement literally. They are listed rather than buried.

### 20.1 The material-move gate applies window-wide

As specified, the gate compared only the endpoint difference. A stock that spiked
12% and round-tripped has `|excess| ≈ 0` and would be demoted out of critical —
silencing the exact case `z_path` exists to catch, which is the specification's
own headline demo. The gate now reads
`max(|excess|, largest single-day |excess| in the window)`.

### 20.2 The same-session branch measures span, not elapsed fraction

`session_span(anchor, now)` rather than the fraction of the session elapsed since
the open. A 15:50 → 15:55 recheck spans five minutes of trading; treating it as a
whole day would flatten a genuine intraday move into noise. The stated acceptance
test for `session_fraction_elapsed` still passes — that function is used for
close-anchored windows, where it is exactly right.

### 20.3 52-week extremes are carried per row

Per [§19](#19-how-the-build-evolved) and [§6](#6-data-model): comparing against a
record the window itself set collapses a continuous signal into a binary touch.

### 20.4 A local HS256 dev issuer exists

`docker compose up` with no keys has to boot a fully working product, and JWKS
verification cannot work without a project. It is a first-class path with its own
screen rather than a hidden fallback, because the per-user isolation claims are
not demonstrable without one. It refuses to run in live provider mode, says what
it is on the login page, announces itself in `/healthz`, and disappears entirely
once a project is configured.

### Two smaller notes

The acceptance criterion "one −20% day moves σ_idio by < 15%" is reached about 21
sessions after the shock, not immediately. The immediate figure is +42%, against
+871% unwinsorised. The test asserts both numbers and names the horizon rather
than quietly redefining the criterion.

Twelve Data's `/time_series` volume-adjustment behaviour on splits remains
**unverified**. It does not matter for this build because there is no
auto-rebase, but it would matter for one.

## 21. Designed, not built

Each of these has a worked design and was cut deliberately. Listing them is the
point: an unlisted omission is indistinguishable from an oversight.

| Cut | Why |
|---|---|
| Notifications of any kind | Push, email and digest scheduling are a product surface of their own, and the read-receipt mechanic has to be right first |
| Two-factor regression (SPY *and* sector, orthogonalised) | A second factor buys accuracy on names whose sector is a poor proxy, at the cost of a second β, a second shrinkage rule and a decomposition panel nobody can read |
| ECDF scoring | Residuals are collected, stored and displayed; scoring still uses the Gaussian z. Replacing it is a calibration project, and the honest interim is to *report* the gap |
| Vasicek cross-sectional shrinkage | Pooling σ across a sector would help thin baselines; the per-ticker prior already covers the same failure at a fraction of the complexity |
| Corporate-action auto-rebase | Cut on safety as much as scope — see [§9.3](#93-corporate-actions-detect-and-freeze-never-auto-rebase) |
| Multiple watchlists | One column on `watchlist_items`, and a great deal of UI. The scoring story does not change |
| A middle polling tier | See [§9.4](#94-the-intraday-layer--two-tiers-not-three) |
| Worker lease / leader election | `pg_advisory_lock` is unsafe through pgbouncer transaction mode; a pooler-safe lease buys nothing at one worker |
| HMAC key rotation | A `kid` header and a two-key verifier. Real, and irrelevant until there is something to rotate |
| Quarterly sector re-checks | Sector reclassification mid-window is *detected*; scheduling a re-check is a job, not a design question |
| Leveraged-ETF handling | A 2x fund needs its own β treatment; the honest interim is that they are not in the benchmark universe |
| **Extended-hours scoring** | The price is displayed, explicitly unscored: `AH −8.2% · not scored until the open` |
| Dividend adjustment | `adjust=splits` only. A dividend gap is a real return the user experienced |
| Learned signal weights | The four weights are hand-set and published. Learning them needs labels, and precision@critical is where those labels would come from |
| Non-US markets | Both free tiers are US-only |
